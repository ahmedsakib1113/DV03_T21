CHART ANALYSIS

Hospitalisations for road traffic injuries increased from 34,033 in 2011 to 39,505 in 2021, an overall rise of 16.1%. The highest annual count was 39,866 in 2019. Counts fell to 37,966 in 2020 before increasing again in 2021.

The dashed line shows the annual average of 37,353.1 hospitalisations across the 11 years. The figures show a generally upward trend with a decline in 2020; they do not establish what caused the changes.

CHART ANALYSIS

The cyclist share of hospitalisations rose from 15.8% in 2011 to 21.9% in 2020, before falling to 20.7% in 2021. The 2021 share remained above both 2011 and 2019, when it was 18.3%.

Over the same period, the vehicle-occupant share fell from approximately 52.0% to 49.4%, while the motorcyclist share remained around 22–23%. A change in share reflects a group's position within the annual total and does not necessarily mean its count changed in the same direction.

CHART ANALYSIS

During 2016–2021, cyclists accounted for 19.02% of hospitalisations but 11.13% of bed days. Pedestrians accounted for 6.49% of hospitalisations and 13.14% of bed days, while motorcyclists accounted for 22.27% and 25.02% respectively.

A higher bed-day share than hospitalisation share indicates a longer average hospital stay relative to the overall average. Bed days measure hospital resource use; they do not provide a complete measure of injury severity because length of stay also depends on treatment, other health conditions, and discharge arrangements.

SOURCE & CONTEXT

What should you know
about these numbers?

These visualisations examine the five research questions using supplied BITRE hospitalisation data. The explanations summarise the displayed patterns and the limits of each comparison.

Read the preliminary investigation ↗
How are averages calculated?

Averages use the displayed observations after applying the shared filters: all 11 years for Q1, selected years for other trend views, and displayed groups for comparison views, and published jurisdictions for Q2. The sex chart averages the matching annual counts for each displayed classification. E1 excludes years with no matching cases when calculating mean shares. E2 averages its displayed changes. Each chart labels its own mean and period; calculations use full precision.

How should comparisons be interpreted?

These are counts and shares, rather than population-standardised or exposure-adjusted risks. Age bands have unequal widths. The map supports year plus one demographic filter and labels suppressed values as not published. Jurisdiction totals can differ from national totals because of unrecorded or migratory/offshore residence. The sex chart retains classifications outside male and female under Other / not recorded. Pie shares use the matching total within their displayed classification. Bed days measure hospital resource use and are not a direct measure of injury severity.

The source notes a series break for Victoria from 2012 and New South Wales from June 2017. These changes should be considered when interpreting national trends.
