YIBO’S EXPLANATION

In 2021, Victoria recorded the highest number of hospitalised road traffic injuries (11,130), followed by Queensland (10,447) and New South Wales (9,003). Together, these three jurisdictions accounted for approximately 77.4% of the national count. The territories recorded substantially lower counts, with the Northern Territory reporting 710 and the Australian Capital Territory 740. These figures are absolute counts rather than population-standardised rates. Therefore, lower counts in smaller jurisdictions do not necessarily indicate lower individual risk.

YIBO’S EXPLANATION

Male hospitalisations remained substantially higher than female hospitalisations throughout 2011–2021. Male cases increased from 22,321 in 2011 to 25,788 in 2021, while female cases increased from 11,712 to 13,716. Males consistently accounted for approximately two-thirds of hospitalisations. The 2020 decline was much larger among females: female hospitalisations fell from 14,499 in 2019 to 12,781 in 2020, while the male count declined only slightly from 25,365 to 25,178. These are counts, not sex-specific population rates or exposure-adjusted risks.

E2 / FURTHER ANALYSIS

How did hospitalisations change by age and road user between 2011 and 2021?

Click or tap to highlight – – Average

Loading interactive chart…

Select a mark to explore its value.

Change in hospitalisations · 2021 minus 2011 · Source: Yibo / BITRE

View values and download data

YIBO’S EXPLANATION

Between 2011 and 2021, growth among older adults was concentrated among car drivers and pedal cyclists rather than pedestrians. Among people aged 40–64, cyclist hospitalisations increased by 1,376 and car-driver hospitalisations by 564, whereas pedestrian hospitalisations decreased by 11. Cyclist increases were also evident among ages 65–74 (+509) and 75+ (+198). In contrast, pedestrian changes among older groups were small or negative: +8 for ages 65–74 and −77 for ages 75+. The findings indicate that the increase among older adults was primarily associated with cycling and driving, rather than pedestrian injuries. The largest decline occurred among pedestrians aged 17–25 (−194), while car-driver hospitalisations increased most among ages 26–39 (+781). These are changes in absolute counts. Population ageing and changes in travel exposure are not controlled for, so the results do not demonstrate changes in individual risk.

YIBO’S EXPLANATION

The change was not uniform across road users. Cyclists increased from 15.8% of hospitalisations in 2011 to 21.9% in 2020. Although the share fell slightly to 20.7% in 2021, it remained above both 2019 (18.3%) and 2011. Vehicle occupants fell from approximately 52.0% to 49.4%, while motorcyclists remained relatively stable at around 22%–23%. The 2020 disruption therefore coincided with a lasting elevation in the cyclist share.

YIBO’S EXPLANATION

The groups contributing the most hospitalisations did not always contribute the same share of bed days. Cyclists accounted for 19.02% of hospitalisations but only 11.13% of bed days. In contrast, pedestrians accounted for only 6.49% of hospitalisations but 13.14% of bed days. Motorcyclists also had a higher bed-day share (25.02%) than hospitalisation share (22.27%). Road-user results showed a similar mismatch. Pedestrians accounted for 6.49% of hospitalisations but 13.14% of bed days, whereas cyclists accounted for 19.02% of hospitalisations but only 11.13% of bed days. Therefore, groups with the most hospitalisations were not necessarily those generating the greatest hospital burden.

E3B / FURTHER ANALYSIS

Do age groups with more hospitalisations also account for more bed days?

Shares of hospitalisations and bed days (%) · 2016–2021 · Source: Yibo / BITRE

YIBO’S EXPLANATION

From 2016 to 2021, people aged 40–64 accounted for the largest shares of both hospitalisations (32.82%) and bed days (34.17%). However, older age groups showed a disproportionate bed-day burden. People aged 75 and over represented only 6.23% of hospitalisations but 13.29% of bed days, while those aged 65–74 accounted for 7.31% of hospitalisations but 11.18% of bed days. In contrast, people aged 8–16 represented 6.61% of hospitalisations but only 3.53% of bed days. These results suggest that hospitalisations among older adults tend to consume more hospital resources relative to their case numbers. Bed days indicate healthcare resource use, but they should not be interpreted as a complete measure of injury severity because length of stay may also be affected by treatment needs, comorbidities, discharge arrangements, and other factors.

How are averages calculated?

Averages are arithmetic means of the displayed values: Q1 across 11 years; Q2 across eight jurisdictions; Q3 across seven known age groups; Q4 across 11 years separately for each sex; Q5 across five road-user groups, including “Other or unknown”. Supplemental percentage charts use a separate mean for each displayed series. E2 averages the 28 displayed changes. Values are rounded for display; calculations use full precision.

How should comparisons be interpreted?

These are counts and shares, rather than population-standardised or exposure-adjusted risks. Age bands have unequal widths. The Q2 map shows 2021 counts; larger or darker regions do not indicate greater individual risk. The eight jurisdiction counts total 39,035; the national total is 39,505 because it also includes unrecorded or migratory/offshore residence. The supplied male and female counts sum to one fewer case than the 2021 national total. Bed days measure hospital resource use rather than injury severity alone.

The source notes a series break for Victoria from 2012 and New South Wales from June 2017. These changes should be considered when interpreting national trends.
